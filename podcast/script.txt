This is AI News Weekly, presented by Roger Basler de Roca. It is Sunday, November 29, 2026, and this week has one pattern running through almost every story. AI is now writing code, approving code, reviewing code and moving markets. The question that keeps coming back is a simple one: who checks the work, and on what basis?

We start with the AI News of the Week.

Nvidia has brought back Jensen Huang as CEO. VentureBeat reports that the board voted unanimously to reinstate him eleven days after announcing his resignation, and only six days after it had publicly ruled out his return. The board says it acted after input from senior US government officials, who indicated that his return poses no national security risk. Reuters reported that Nvidia shares were up more than thirty percent by late morning. What has not changed is the investigation. It was opened on November 8, and the board says it will be completed. The announcement also says nothing about his compensation or about the financing exposures that remain open. So the company has its leader back, but the governance question that started all of this is still unanswered. For anyone running an organisation, this is a reminder that a key person can be both the source of value and the largest single risk at the same time.

The second story is about Anthropic, which listed on Nasdaq only last week. TechCrunch reports that the stock traded as low as seventy six dollars on Tuesday. That is below the IPO price of eighty three dollars and nearly twelve percent under Monday's close, less than a week after the shares opened at one hundred eleven dollars fifty. Investors now treat Anthropic's share price as a barometer for how public markets value AI in general. TechCrunch notes that if the weak trading continues, it could put pressure on the valuations of private AI startups. One week of trading proves little. But it shows that the public market has started to ask the question that private rounds rarely ask: what is this growth actually worth?

The third story is a deal. According to Bloomberg, as cited by TechCrunch, Amazon is paying seven and a half billion dollars for the technology behind Decart's Optimization Stack, together with the founding team and its researchers. The software helps AI models run efficiently on Nvidia, Amazon and Google hardware. Amazon has so far used its own Trainium chips only in its own data centers and is preparing to sell them to customers. The logic is that the competition with Nvidia is not only about silicon, it is about how much useful work you get out of every chip. The deal is reported, not confirmed, and neither company commented.

Let's move to AI Tools, Startups, and Innovations.

Anthropic has launched Claude Ship, a set of agents that review, fix and approve code changes. TechCrunch reports that Code Review is generally available, while PR Steward and Claude Approvals are in beta. The interesting part is Claude Approvals. A team writes its approval policy in plain language as a file in the repository, and the agent applies that policy to routine changes. Anthropic itself recommends starting in observe-only mode, beginning with a single repository and setting monthly spending limits. That recommendation is the real message. The tool can approve, but the organisation still has to decide what deserves approval, and write that down.

The second tool story is quieter but matters for every company that connects AI agents to outside systems. VentureBeat reports that Skills over MCP is now in public beta in Claude and Claude Code. In practice, a connected server can now supply agent skills, not only tools. The specification itself names server-supplied skills as a prompt-injection risk. Each skill needs explicit approval before it can run code locally, and if its files change, it has to be approved again. The specification is still labelled beta. For IT and security teams, this changes what they approve when they connect a server. They are no longer approving a connector, they are approving behaviour that can change over time.

The third tool comes from OpenAI and Cisco. On the OpenAI blog, they introduce Shirudo, an open-source runtime for Linux that uses eBPF to apply temporary protections, called shields, to running systems. In a controlled test, a shield generated with Codex blocked an exploit attempt against a running Elasticsearch service without a restart. A Trusted Platform Module keeps a verifiable record of which protections were loaded or removed. The evidence so far is one controlled test, and a technical deep dive is announced for December 15. The idea is still worth noting: when attacks speed up, protection needs to be applied faster, and it needs an audit trail.

Now to Regulation and Ethics.

TechCrunch reports that a coalition of forty two state attorneys general is intervening in the bankruptcy of Character.AI. The company filed for Chapter 11 in October and plans to auction a dataset that, according to the attorneys general, contains chat histories from more than twenty one million US accounts, over two point one million of them belonging to minors. The auction is scheduled for December 1. Character.AI says user information would still be protected under its privacy policy, and it is unclear what the buyer plans to do with the chats. This case makes visible something that is easy to overlook. Conversations with an AI system are an asset on the balance sheet, and in a bankruptcy, assets are sold.

The second regulation story is a statement, not a rule. The Register reports that President Trump said, after the APEC summit, that US companies will not be allowed to build datacenters outside the country, and threatened a tremendous tax on overseas builds. He added that he had not even told his own staff yet. Omdia's chief analyst points out that there is no such thing as a purely American datacenter, because they are built from components sourced all over the world. And with Democrats controlling both houses of Congress from January, such a policy is seen as unlikely. For companies planning infrastructure abroad, the lesson is not to react to the headline, but to account for political volatility in long-term decisions.

The third story comes from India. The Register reports that Prime Minister Narendra Modi described an AI app that would collect data on children from the age of three and steer them toward study or career paths. Uttar Pradesh, a state with more than two hundred forty million residents, has published a one hundred seventy eight page tender for an AI Stack linked to the state's Family ID. Its use cases include AI-enabled social media monitoring. The Internet Freedom Foundation says that no legislation explicitly authorises these systems and that the tender leaves out important oversight measures. These are proposals, not adopted policy. But they show that the same technology that makes administration more efficient also makes surveillance cheaper, and that the difference lies in the rules around it.

Next, Voices and Perspectives.

OpenAI's chief scientist, Jakub Pachocki, published a post called Perplexity and Intelligence. He proposes using perplexity, essentially the loss a model reaches in pretraining, as a shared measure to compare developers and to inform common safety standards. OpenAI is publishing its own internal measurements as a first step. The post suggests that labs could agree on common limits that they commit not to cross. The post is also honest about the limits: perplexity does not capture the gains from chain-of-thought reasoning, and comparing labs is hard. It is a proposal for a standard, not yet a standard. But it is a rare attempt to define a measurable line instead of a vague promise.

The second voice is louder. The Register reports that Yann LeCun attacked Anthropic's CEO Dario Amodei on X, accusing him of hyping the dangers of AI so that open models would be made illegal. Amodei, in a blog post, writes that Anthropic has never advocated a ban on open-weights models, while also saying that such models potentially present a higher risk than closed ones. The Register adds a practical detail. Revolut cut its vibe coding costs tenfold by moving about two thousand employees from Claude to Z.AI's GLM model, running on its own Nvidia hardware. The debate about open and closed models is often framed as philosophy. This example shows that for companies it is also a question of price and dependency.

The third voice is a philosopher. MIT Technology Review reports that Anthropic, before going public, named the possibility that AI could be conscious as a risk factor for investors. The philosopher Thomas Nagel calls the idea ridiculous. His argument is that reproducing what a brain does is not the same as reproducing what it experiences. A recent poll cited in the article finds that thirty three percent of Americans believe AI will definitely or probably become conscious. The article concludes that this remains a philosophical question for now. For decision makers, the relevant point is not who is right. It is that public belief is moving faster than the science, and that belief shapes regulation and trust.

Finally, Implications for Business and Society.

VentureBeat reports on the LeadDev AI Code Review Report. Forty seven percent of respondents say their organisations rely on AI coding agents to review the code those same agents wrote. Only twenty six percent are very confident that they understand AI-generated changes as thoroughly as human ones. Only fourteen percent say they have clear, consistently applied standards for reviewing AI code. The survey covers three hundred seventy seven engineers and leaders in the US and Europe, and it was sponsored by CodeRabbit, a vendor of AI code review. Even with that caveat, the numbers describe a gap. Production has accelerated, but understanding has not kept up.

The second business story is a cost test. VentureBeat ran several models on a real eight thousand four hundred line codebase with five bug reports. Claude Fable 5.2 passed ten of fifteen attempts for about eighty nine dollars. Claude Opus 5.5 passed nine of fifteen for about twenty nine dollars. That is eight dollars ninety per success against three dollars twenty two. VentureBeat stresses that a one-pass difference is too small to call, and that the results describe this codebase, not coding ability in general. Still, the test shows a pattern that matters for budgets. The most capable model is not automatically the most economical one, and the choice of model is an operating decision.

The last story is about energy. MIT Technology Review reports that by October 2026, developers had announced more than one hundred gas power plants built directly for US data centers, with over one hundred fifteen gigawatts of planned capacity. According to Cleanview, only four point nine gigawatts of that is operating today. BloombergNEF estimates that if all of them are built, they could emit one hundred fifty two million tonnes of CO2 equivalent more per year by 2035 than if the data centers drew power from the grid. The article notes that not all of these plants may be built. But the direction is clear. Waiting five to seven years for a grid connection is too slow for the AI build-out, so companies build their own power. The cost of speed shows up somewhere else.

So what connects these stories? AI now produces, reviews and approves work faster than organisations can understand it. Nvidia's board, Anthropic's investors, engineering teams and attorneys general are all confronting the same issue from different sides. Capability is available. Verification is the scarce resource. Whoever decides what gets checked, and who does the checking, decides how much of that capability can actually be trusted.
