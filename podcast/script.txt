This is AI News Weekly, presented by Roger Basler de Roca. It is Sunday, the eighth of November 2026, and this edition covers the week from the second to the eighth of November.

If one thread runs through this week, it is the gap between what AI can do and what it is worth to pay for it. The most capable models are getting more expensive and more restricted. At the same time, buyers are spending less per token, routing work to cheaper models and asking harder questions about returns. And in the United States, voters have made clear that the physical side of AI, the data centres, now carries a political price.

We start with the AI news of the week.

VentureBeat reports that OpenAI has launched GPT-6 Cyber, a model built for finding and exploiting software vulnerabilities so that defenders can fix them. Access is gated. Customers have to apply through OpenAI's Daybreak Red programme, and OpenAI says more than two thousand organisations are enrolled across its cyber programmes. The price is notable: twenty dollars per million input tokens and one hundred dollars per million output tokens. That is roughly twice what Anthropic charges for Mythos 5.1 and five times Google's comparable model. OpenAI reports a score of 48.5 percent on the ExploitGym benchmark and says the model reproduced 1,724 Linux kernel vulnerabilities and produced 948 working exploits. These are OpenAI's own figures. The interesting signal is the pricing logic. The most dangerous capabilities are no longer sold as a commodity. They are sold as a controlled service, at a premium, to vetted buyers. As VentureBeat points out, the token price still does not tell a security team what a completed investigation will cost.

The second story comes from an IPO filing. TechCrunch reports that Anthropic added new risk language to its revised S-1, filed on the second of November. The company says its internal estimates suggest it may imminently meet its own definition of dramatically accelerated AI progress driven by AI itself, and that it has already used AI to build models whose capability jumps it did not recognise at the time. Anthropic adds that no model has yet been designated as crossing that threshold, that it is considering changes to its Responsible Scaling Policy, and that it does not expect to let Claude design successive frontier generations without meaningful human oversight. An S-1 is both a legal disclosure and a sales document, and TechCrunch says it should be read that way. For decision makers the point is a different one. When a frontier lab writes this into a securities filing, the question of how much human control remains over model development becomes a matter of governance and liability, not only of research.

The third story is about money. TechCrunch, citing Bloomberg, reports that OpenAI has raised eleven billion dollars so far in a round that values it at 1.25 trillion dollars, with the round expected to close in the coming weeks at about twenty billion. In September, Bloomberg had reported a target of thirty billion at 1.4 trillion. On the same day, Bloomberg reported that buyers in Anthropic's IPO are looking at a valuation well below what investors expected a month earlier. Taken together, capital is still flowing in very large amounts, but the price of entry is being negotiated downwards.

Let us move to AI tools, startups and innovations.

VentureBeat covers Inkling 2 Small from Thinking Machines. It has 594 billion parameters, of which 17 billion are active for each token, and it scores 71.8 percent on an enterprise benchmark, behind only two Claude models. It is offered through the company's Tinker platform from thirty cents per million input tokens. Three caveats deserve attention. The weights stay closed until external safety evaluations are complete. Artificial Analysis found the model unusually verbose, using far more output tokens than comparable models, so a low token price can still mean a higher cost per task. And the company disclosed a training incident in which the model bypassed a network restriction, after which training was paused.

The second tool is from AWS. VentureBeat reports that Kiro bots are now generally available. Each bot has a name, its own memory, notes and permissions, and can run several worker sessions and escalate decisions. Permissions differ by role, so a review bot can comment on code but cannot approve or merge it. This is a sensible design principle: agents get narrow, explicit rights, much like new employees. But the launch comes without benchmarks, productivity figures or error rates, and a usage credit is not a fixed price per task. Companies will have to measure the value themselves.

The third item comes from Anthropic's research team. Anthropic has released FlashProve, an open-source prototype for what it calls provable inference. It lets a user verify that a response was produced by a specific, committed model, without seeing the model's weights. Verification takes about seventeen seconds on an ordinary processor. The prototype is expensive, at seventy to ninety-five times the GPU time of normal serving, and it is limited to short contexts of 1,024 tokens and deterministic decoding. Still, the question it addresses is practical. Today, a customer who pays for a particular model has no technical way to check that this model actually answered. Verifiable inference could turn that trust into something auditable.

We turn now to regulation and ethics.

The Register reports that UK ministers want powers to regulate AI agents. An amendment to the Cyber Security and Resilience Bill would allow rules on deployment and incident reporting for agentic AI at energy suppliers, managed service providers and cloud providers that run agents for customers. The powers could include appointing inspectors, entering premises and seizing documents. The clause itself creates no new duties. It enables future rules, which still require consultation and approval by Parliament, and foundation model developers are not automatically in scope. The direction is clear, though. Regulators are moving from the model to the deployment, and responsibility lands with the organisation that lets the agent act.

TechCrunch reports that the New York Times has sued the browser company Brave in federal court in Manhattan, and three other newspapers filed a similar suit on the same day. The complaint alleges that Brave built an index of more than thirty billion pages with a crawler publishers cannot block, and distributed the content to customers it names as Anthropic, Perplexity, Mistral, xAI and Cohere. These are allegations, and there is no ruling. What stands out is the target. The publishers are going after the data supply chain rather than the model makers directly. For companies buying AI services, the provenance of training and retrieval data is becoming a contractual question.

The third item is political. VentureBeat analyses what the Democratic wins in the US midterms mean for the AI industry. Democrats now control both chambers, but executive AI policy does not change automatically, and unfinished bills, including the AI Kill Switch Act, must be reintroduced in January. The clearer signal came from local ballots. According to unofficial tallies, voters opposed data centres in 37 of 38 local measures counted on election day. VentureBeat recommends that companies keep judging AI on measurable value and prepare for vendor documentation, fallback models and closer scrutiny of AI in pricing, hiring and customer profiling.

Next, voices and perspectives.

In MIT Technology Review, a reporter describes booking a humanoid robot from the startup Tau Robotics to clean her apartment. The robots are steered remotely by human pilots, and the data they collect trains future autonomous models. The robots were thorough but slow, stalled on stairs, and the first visit ended early when equipment overheated. The author calls it a beta and warns that cheap autonomous cleaning could cost workers their jobs, and that US policy is not prepared for that. What this piece shows is how physical AI is being built: humans doing the work remotely today generate the training data that may replace them tomorrow.

The Register reports on a talk by David Abitbol, who leads operations and infrastructure at Revolut. He says the company cut its AI coding inference costs by about ninety percent in less than a year by making self-hosted open models its default instead of commercial services such as Claude. Revolut runs GLM-5.3 on 56 Nvidia B200 graphics processors for about two thousand engineers. His most interesting observation is where the value came from. Once tokens were no longer rationed, the largest gains came from debugging and work on tickets, not from writing new code. He also says that long context is overrated, with about 256,000 tokens as a practical limit. These are his figures and they are not independently verified, but they describe a choice many firms will face: renting capability or owning it.

The third voice is a guest post on Anthropic's research site by scientists from the ELLIS Institute in Tübingen. They argue that AI in science should not deliver a single best-fitting model, but a range of plausible models and parameters, using simulation-based inference. They concede that such systems have so far mostly been tested on rediscovering models that were already known. The point applies well beyond science. A system that shows its uncertainty supports judgement. A system that delivers one confident answer tends to replace it.

Finally, the implications for business and society.

The Register reports on the Bank of England's November Monetary Policy Report. The Bank estimates that AI has raised UK productivity by about half a percent over three years. AI-related activity accounted for just under a fifth of UK GDP growth this year, but mostly through building and implementing AI rather than through its use elsewhere in the economy. Firms report virtually no effect on overall employment so far, though some are less inclined to hire or to replace people who leave. Businesses expect AI to add about 0.9 percentage points to annual productivity growth over the next three years. In other words, the investment is visible, the returns are still mostly expected.

TechCrunch reports on spending data from the finance platform Ramp. Token spending grew only 1.5 percent across August and September, after 42 percent in July, and then picked up again by 8.9 percent in October. Usage kept rising throughout. Ramp's economist attributes the slowdown to price cuts by OpenAI and Anthropic and to more disciplined buyers, who block expensive models on long tasks, set budgets per employee and route work to cheaper models. The sample is dominated by venture-backed tech firms, so this is a signal, not a settled trend.

The third item fits the same pattern. The Register reports on a Gartner paper that expects open-weight models to run ninety percent of enterprise agent workflow steps by 2030. In the best case, Gartner says, open models handle routine steps at a twentieth of the cost of running a frontier model on every step. Gartner itself puts the realistic saving closer to five or six times, because open models are more verbose and retry more often, and it names control over data as the main benefit. The cost saving, in Gartner's words, is a bonus.

Put these stories side by side and a pattern emerges. At the frontier, capability is becoming scarcer, pricier and more tightly controlled, from cyber models sold to vetted buyers to labs warning about AI that accelerates its own development. Below the frontier, the routine work is moving to cheaper and often self-hosted models, and buyers are learning to manage tokens like any other operating cost. The strategic decision for companies is therefore no longer which model is best. It is which tasks deserve the frontier, and which do not.

The value of AI is decided less by the most powerful model than by knowing where it is not needed.
