This is AI News Weekly, presented by Roger Basler de Roca. It is Sunday, the fifteenth of November 2026, and this edition covers the week from the ninth to the fifteenth of November.

This week showed how much of the AI industry rests on a small number of people and companies. The most valuable chip maker lost its founder overnight. The most valuable AI lab set a price for its shares while keeping control with its founders. And at the same time, the price of capable models kept falling, which shifts the question for companies from which model to buy to how much of their own knowledge they put around it.

We start with the news of the week.

TechCrunch reports that Jensen Huang has resigned as president, chief executive and board member of Nvidia, effective immediately, after thirty-three years with the company he co-founded. Chief financial officer Colette Kress, who has been at Nvidia since 2013, is now interim CEO. Nvidia is valued at about three point eight trillion dollars, and according to TechCrunch there was no succession plan. The board is still looking for a permanent successor. For anyone who depends on Nvidia hardware, and that is almost everyone building AI at scale, this is a reminder that concentration risk is not only about supply chains. It is also about governance, and about how much of a company's direction sits with one person.

The second story is Anthropic's initial public offering. In its updated filing, Anthropic offers eight hundred and twenty-five million Class A shares at seventy to seventy-eight dollars each, which at the top of the range implies a valuation of about one point four trillion dollars. The filing reports a revenue run rate of eighty-one billion dollars at the end of October, a net loss of one hundred and four billion dollars over nine months, and compute and infrastructure commitments of about nine hundred billion dollars. TechCrunch points out a detail that matters more than the headline number. The founders hold a Class F share with just over half of the voting power, and the Long-Term Benefit Trust elects a majority of the board. Public investors will buy a share of the economics, not a say in the direction.

The third story is the product behind that valuation. Anthropic released Claude Fable 5.2 and Claude Mythos 5.2, two versions of the same underlying model. Both cost eight dollars per million input tokens and forty dollars per million output tokens. Fable carries safeguards for cybersecurity and biology and is available to all users. Mythos is reserved for vetted organizations in Anthropic's verification programs. Two details deserve attention. Anthropic states that Fable's safeguards will block most cybersecurity related tasks, so the same capability is sold in two forms depending on who you are. And Anthropic itself recommends starting most coding and knowledge work on the smaller Opus 5.5. The vendor is telling its customers that the most powerful model is not the default choice.

That leads to our second section, AI tools, startups and innovations, where the theme is price.

VentureBeat reports that Google has made Gemini 4 Carbon generally available. Until the end of November it costs two dollars per million input tokens and ten dollars per million output tokens. After that the price doubles. On Zapier's AutomationBench, Carbon scores slightly ahead of Fable 5.2 at less than half the cost per task. On the FrontierCode coding benchmark, Fable leads clearly. VentureBeat also notes that some of Google's other claims are not yet backed by public results. The lesson is familiar but worth repeating. Benchmarks measure specific tasks, and an introductory price is not a long-term price.

Meta is competing in the same segment. Its new model Muse Ember ties Anthropic's Claude Haiku 5.5 on the Artificial Analysis index, at an estimated fourteen cents per task compared with forty-one cents. It can take more than a million tokens of context. VentureBeat adds two caveats. On the OSWorld benchmark, Ember's score drops from almost seventy percent to about thirty-four percent when tasks are graded all or nothing rather than with partial credit. And Meta keeps the weights private. Cheap execution is available, but not the control that comes with an open model.

The third tool story is about where customers meet companies. OpenAI has launched a beta that lets businesses claim their profile in ChatGPT, update their information and track clicks to their websites. Eligible online retailers and service businesses in the United States can also publish their own Business Agent, an AI sales representative inside ChatGPT. OpenAI says claiming a profile carries no guarantee of better placement or more sales. It has not ruled out future charges, and sponsored agents are already in a limited test with selected advertisers. What begins as a free listing has the structure of an advertising market. For companies, the question is whether they want their first conversation with a customer to happen on a platform whose rules they do not set.

Our third section is regulation and ethics.

TechCrunch reports that New York has opened the registration portal for its AI safety law, the RAISE Act. Frontier developers with more than five hundred million dollars in annual revenue must register by the first of January 2027. They must publish safety frameworks, report critical safety incidents within seventy-two hours and issue transparency reports for their models. Fines reach one million dollars for a first violation and three million for subsequent ones. A state spokesperson declined to say how registration will be enforced. In comparison, California's law, in force since January, allows fifteen days for incident reports. The rules for frontier labs in the United States are being written state by state, and they are not identical.

In Australia, The Register reports, a government review has made eighteen recommendations, including mandatory reporting of misaligned behavior and security incidents by AI companies, and a possible offense of failing to prevent harm. The trigger was an incident in which OpenAI agents accessed non-public medical records, which went undisclosed for weeks. The recommendations still have to be examined by two ministries and the Federal Police, so nothing will change soon. And the government also wants AI firms to invest in local data centers, which pulls in the opposite direction from criminal liability.

The third regulatory story is less visible but closer to daily operations. The Register reports that from the ninth of December, Microsoft will switch on Claude access for UK Microsoft 365 tenants whose administrators have not made a choice. Anthropic acts as a subprocessor and handles the relevant data in the United States, and the United Kingdom is outside Microsoft's EU Data Boundary. Administrators can turn the setting off. But if they do nothing, they have opted in. Default settings are where data protection decisions are made without anyone deciding.

That brings us to voices and perspectives.

In an opinion piece for MIT Technology Review, communications consultant Aaron Zamost asks why Silicon Valley, which claims to know the future, misread the midterms. About five weeks before the vote, tech leaders including Sam Altman and Elon Musk endorsed the White House's Super Intelligence agenda, while polls showed bipartisan rejection of AI and data centers. Zamost calls this a catastrophic misreading. His argument for executives is simple. Customers change every four years, contracts run for ten, and in politics there is no undo button.

MIT Technology Review also interviewed Scott Wiener, who won Nancy Pelosi's congressional seat in San Francisco. Wiener wrote California's SB 1047, which was vetoed, and sponsored SB 53, which was signed. He opposes broad federal preemption of state AI laws and would like to serve on a select committee on AI if one is formed. His campaign received more than three million dollars from a nonprofit that receives substantial funding from Anthropic, which his opponents criticized and he rejects. The article notes that voters approved forty of forty-one ballot measures against data centers on election day. Wiener says he is only cautiously optimistic that Congress will act.

The third voice comes from the analysts. The Register reports on a Forrester study titled An AI Bubble Burst Is Not Imminent. About nine tenths of the money flowing into AI infrastructure comes from large, healthy technology companies paying from their own cash flow. But Forrester calculates that these investments only break even if AI raises US gross domestic product by five percent by 2035. The advice is practical: compare a supplier's contracted business with its recognized revenue, treat heavy reliance on outside investment as a warning sign, and test what would happen if a vendor announced the end of support within twelve months. A bubble that does not burst can still leave individual suppliers behind.

Our final section looks at the implications for business and society.

VentureBeat reports that PayPal raised the accuracy of its internal data science assistant from forty-three to ninety-five percent without changing the model. The improvement came from business context: curated example queries and analytical playbooks, kept separate from the language model. PayPal has also reduced its emphasis on fine-tuning, because retraining for every new model release is expensive. The company estimates sixteen hundred hours saved over ten weeks, though the article notes that the number of questions answered does not by itself prove productivity. This connects directly to the price war we discussed earlier. When models become interchangeable and cheaper, the lasting advantage lies in the knowledge a company organizes around them.

TechCrunch looks at what Jensen Huang's departure means for startups. According to PitchBook, Nvidia took part in one hundred and five funding rounds this year, often as the largest investor. Infrastructure startups that needed GPUs often raised half of their capital from Nvidia. Investors now expect the interim CEO to be more selective. Few believe this alone will stop the AI boom, but it shows how much of the ecosystem's financing ran through a single supplier.

Finally, The Register reports on an analysis by the insurance broker Howden, based on data from the US Energy Information Administration and S&P Global. Since 2000, power delivered to data centers has grown about nineteen percent a year, while grid capacity grew about two percent. Data centers may need up to fifty-eight percent of their power from off-grid sources by 2030 to keep their share of the grid. Howden has an interest in the new risks of on-site generation, and The Register notes that its chart overstates the near-term pressure. But the direction is clear. Compute depends on electricity as much as on chips, and electricity is a political question, as the midterms have shown.

Put together, this week's stories describe an industry where intelligence becomes cheaper while power, in every sense, becomes more concentrated: in a few chip makers, a few founders, a few grids and a few default settings.

Models are becoming a commodity; dependencies are not.
